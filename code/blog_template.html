Wikidata knows, for some places, what they were named after. I mapped every settlement it says is named after another place, {{source.n|int}} of them, and drew a line from each namesake to the place that borrowed its name.

Boston in Massachusetts is named after Boston in Lincolnshire, {{ex.boston.km|int}} km away across the Atlantic. Perth in Western Australia is named after Perth in Scotland, {{ex.perth.km|int}} km away. Brooklyn is named after Breukelen, a village near Utrecht. On Kiritimati, an atoll in the middle of the Pacific, there is a Paris and a Poland. Borrowed names are everywhere, and Wikidata, the open database behind Wikipedia, has started to record them in a property called "named after".

I took every settlement that Wikidata says is named after another geographical place. The raw query gave {{source.links_raw|int}} links, but most were not settlements at all: {{source.excluded_stat_links|int}} were US census "urban areas", which Wikidata names after their main town. Once those, and camps, stations, parks and other oddities, were removed, {{source.n|int}} links were left, from {{source.places|int}} places to {{source.namesakes|int}} namesakes.

Mostly the river, or the town next door

The first surprise is how local most names are. Only {{cats.Settlement.pct|int}}% of the places are named after another settlement. The rest borrow from the landscape: {{cats.River.n|int}} from rivers and streams, {{cats.Mountain or hill.n|int}} from mountains and hills, {{lakes|int}} from lakes and seas, and hundreds more from bays, islands, castles, forts and even streets. Half of all the namesakes are less than {{dist.median|f1}} km from the place named after them (counting each link once).

But the distances have two humps. One is the neighbours. The other is the migrants: {{dist.over1000|int}} links are more than 1,000 km long, and {{ocean.n|int}} ({{ocean.pct|int}}%) join two different landmasses. Between the two, the counts dip. A name mostly either stayed at home or crossed an ocean. The longest journeys all end in New Zealand, where {{longest.0.place|raw}} is named after {{longest.0.namesake|raw}} in the {{longest.0.sc|raw}}, {{longest.0.km|int}} km away, almost the far side of the planet.

Britain to the world

{{border.n|int}} links cross a national border, and the United Kingdom gave {{giving.0.n|int}} of them, {{giving.0.pct|int}}%, five times as many as Germany ({{giving.1.n|int}}). {{gbr.four_pct|int}}% of Britain's names went to just four countries: {{gbr.usa|int}} to the United States, {{gbr.aus|int}} to Australia, {{gbr.can|int}} to Canada and {{gbr.nzl|int}} to New Zealand. Smaller streams tell their own stories: Dutch names in what was New Netherland, from Brooklyn to New Utrecht; German, Norwegian and Swedish names across the Midwest; and {{greece.tur_grc|int}} Greek towns, such as Nea Smyrni, named after places in Turkey by refugees in the 1920s.

Prague, Amsterdam and the New Xs

The most borrowed namesake is Prague, with {{top.0.n|int}} places, but most are Czech suburbs called Pražské Předměstí, "Prague suburb". Amsterdam ({{top.1.n|int}}) is the champion abroad: New Amsterdam on Manhattan, Nieuw Amsterdam in Suriname, New Amsterdam in Guyana, and Amsterdams in New York, Missouri, Canada and South Africa. Then come Moscow, Berlin, Paris, Bristol and Windsor.

{{IMG|b_top.png|Bar chart of the fifteen namesakes with the most places named after them: Prague {{top.0.n|int}}, Amsterdam {{top.1.n|int}}, Anatolia, Moscow and Europe 8, then the Sakarya River, Paris, Germany, Berlin, Bristol and the Loire, with darker bar sections for places abroad|The most borrowed names.}}

{{new.n|int}} places are a "New" version of their namesake: New, Nieuw, Neu, Nova, Novo, Nueva. Most are not far from the old one: {{new.under30_pct|int}}% are within 30 km, like Neu-Ulm across the Danube from Ulm, New Delhi beside Delhi, or the new desert cities of Egypt. {{new.ocean|int}} crossed an ocean, from New Amsterdam and New Madrid to Novo Hamburgo in Brazil and Nueva Palmira in Uruguay, named after Palmyra in Syria.

Athens, Rome and Belgium Township

A quarter of all the links are in the United States, and {{us.old|int}} of them are to places outside the Americas: classical names like Athens, Sparta and Syracuse, biblical ones like Bethlehem and Hebron, and the homes of immigrants. Minnesota tops the list with {{us_top_state.n|int}}, but that says more about Wikidata than about Minnesota: most are townships, such as Belgium Township and Christiania Township, whose name origins someone entered in bulk.

A map of who fills in Wikidata

The biggest caveat is coverage. Four countries, the United States, Germany, Australia and the Netherlands, hold {{cov.top4_pct|int}}% of all the links. India has {{cov.ind|int}}, China {{cov.chn|int}}, Mexico {{cov.mex|int}}. The great Spanish naming of the Americas is almost absent: Spain and Portugal gave only {{iberia.latam|int}} names to Latin America in the file. Famous borrowed names like New York City are missing too, because Wikidata does not record them as named after a place with coordinates. So the patterns are real, but this is an early and partial atlas. It will fill in as Wikidata does.

How I did this

One Wikidata query, run on the QLever engine in {{source.fetched|raw}}: every human settlement with a "named after" value that is a geographic location, both with coordinates. I collapsed the repeated rows, removed statistical areas, camps, stations and other non-settlements, and sorted the namesakes into settlements, rivers, regions, mountains and other things from their Wikidata classes. Distances are great circles; borders use Wikidata's own countries; an ocean crossing means the two ends are on different landmasses. The paper sets out every rule and caveat.
